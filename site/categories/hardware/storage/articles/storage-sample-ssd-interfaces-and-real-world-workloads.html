SSD Interfaces and Real-World Workloads

Understand the difference between storage form factors, transport interfaces and the performance that matters for everyday computing.

Separate the connector from the protocol

M.2 describes a family of physical module dimensions and connectors. An M.2 SSD can use SATA or PCI Express depending on the drive and host. NVMe is a protocol designed for non-volatile storage and commonly used over PCIe in personal computers. A physically fitting drive is not necessarily compatible.

Match the workload to the measurement

Sequential transfer rates are relevant to moving large contiguous files, while random access and latency influence application launches and many small-file operations. Queue depth changes benchmark results; a desktop user may not benefit from headline throughput measured under heavy parallel loads.

Look beyond peak write speed

Many drives use a fast write cache that can become exhausted during long transfers. Sustained performance depends on the flash, controller, available capacity and cooling. Compare sustained tests that resemble the intended workload, particularly for video editing or repeated large backups.

Check the platform and thermal limits

Consult the computer or motherboard manual for slot length, supported protocols and PCIe lane arrangements. Some slots share resources with other devices. A compact system may need careful thermal management, since high temperatures can cause a drive to reduce speed.

Capacity and reliability matter

Reserve space for applications, working files and operating-system updates. Compare endurance ratings and warranty terms, but maintain independent backups because no drive is immune to failure. For a basic office PC, sufficient capacity and consistent responsiveness may be more valuable than the fastest available interface.
